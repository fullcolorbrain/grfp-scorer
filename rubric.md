# NSF GRFP Personal Statement Rubric (v1.0)

Ten principles, each scored 0–2. Total out of 20. Derived from reviewer ratings
sheets on 8 ecology/evolution applications (2009–2018), 9 funded psychology and
neuroscience statements (2008–2022), and 24 neuroscience applications (2022–2023
cycles) matched against NSF's official award-offer and honorable-mention lists.

Scoring anchors: 0 = absent or contradicted; 1 = present but partial, generic, or
unsupported; 2 = fully present with specifics.

## The ten principles

1. **Outcomes stated plainly and counted.** Publications (with venue), presentations,
   awards, grants, software adopted — named as facts. "In preparation" and "in review"
   count; forecasting a target journal for unsubmitted work does not, and is a risk.
   Dollar amounts add nothing.
2. **One organizing question or identity in the first paragraph, carried through.**
   The reader knows by the end of paragraph one what the applicant wants to understand,
   and every later section serves it.
3. **Initiative and autonomy narrated as specific acts.** "I called her that day";
   "I was the primary researcher on the collaboration between X and Y." Not "I joined a
   lab and contributed to a project."
4. **Broader impacts as a plan with named programs, partners, audiences, and measures.**
   Past leadership shown with specifics (headcounts, what was built); future commitments
   named (program, institution, role), not "I hope to engage in outreach."
5. **Research tied explicitly to societal benefit.** One clear statement of who,
   outside the field, is better off if the work succeeds. Low discriminating power in
   neuroscience pools; still expected by NSF's criteria.
6. **Explicit Intellectual Merit and Broader Impacts framing.** Required headings
   present; each section actually argues the criterion rather than restating the CV.
7. **Personal narrative converted into evidence of capability.** A non-traditional
   path, an obstacle, or an origin story is used to demonstrate something reviewers
   score — independence, persistence, leadership — rather than left as biography.
8. **One question and one finding per project, in plain language.** Each project gets
   the question, what was found, and what it means, in terms a biologist outside the
   subfield can follow. Technique inventories score 0.
9. **Obstacles narrated as method.** At least one concrete episode of something
   failing and what the applicant did about it, connected to the larger narrative.
10. **A named graduate plan.** Advisor or lab, project, the techniques still to be
    learned, and how the fellowship changes what is possible.

## Supplementary flag (not part of the 20-point total)

**D. Disease/clinical centrality of the research framing.** 0 = none; 1 = disease
mentioned as relevance; 2 = a disease, patient population, or therapy organizes the
narrative. In the 24-application neuroscience pool, 0 of 7 statements scoring 2 were
funded. Score it, report it, and treat 2 as a revision priority.

## Calibration (what the numbers meant in the reference corpora)

| Reference | Total (of 20) | Four-item (of 8) |
|---|---|---|
| Neuroscience awarded/HM (n=8, incl. two held-out) | 12–17, median 14 | 5–8, median 6 |
| Neuroscience unfunded (n=18) | 7–18, median 12 | 2–7, median 5 |
| Ecology, panel-rated Excellent throughout | 16–19 | 6–8 |
| Funded psychology/neuro (Lang, current format) | 13–18 | 4–8 |

The four-item score is P1 + P4 + P7 + P10: the principles that separated funded from
unfunded in the neuroscience pool (item AUCs 0.70, 0.77, 0.65, 0.68). The four-item sum
reaches AUC 0.76 under nested leave-one-out cross-validation, against 0.66 for the full
ten-item total, and transfers to ecology panel ratings (Spearman 0.54). Adding items beyond
four or five adds noise; fitting weights overfits at this sample size.

Read the totals as a floor, not a prediction. Below about 11, the statement itself is
limiting. Above about 14, the outcome depends on the rest of the application.
